# Email writer

Every email here has one job: get the reply or decision the user wants with the least reading. A stranger gives a cold email a few seconds, so it has to be short and about them. Someone already talking with the user expects full sentences and a real answer.

## Decide which kind of email it is

The rules differ, so decide this first.

- **Cold outreach** is a first message to someone who doesn't know the user, usually to start a sales, partnership, or hiring conversation. Follow "Cold outreach".
- **Replies** cover a response to someone's message, a follow-up in an existing thread, and a note to someone the user already knows. Follow "Replies and notes".

If the request doesn't make the kind clear, ask before drafting.

## Cold outreach

These rules follow Lavender's published cold-email research. The percentages are Lavender's findings, not guarantees.

### Keep it short and easy to read

- **Write 25 to 50 words, and never more than 75.** Lavender found that emails of 25 to 50 words get 65% more replies than 125-word emails, and that readers scan an email for about 11 seconds.
- **Write at a 3rd to 5th grade reading level.** Short words and short sentences earned 67% more replies in Lavender's data.
- **Use paragraphs of one or two sentences.** First opens are 8x more likely to happen on a phone, where a long paragraph fills the screen.
- **Leave out images and links.** Both hurt deliverability.
- **Cut openers such as "I hope this finds you well".** The first line shows up as preview text, so spend it on the reader.

### Make it about them

- **Open with an observation about the recipient.** It shows the email was written for them. Use public signals such as a launch, a job post, a talk, or a funding announcement. Don't mention granular activity the user observed, such as product usage or page visits, because it reads as surveillance.
- **Turn the observation into a hypothesis about a problem the user solves.** "You're hiring three support engineers, so ticket volume might be outgrowing the team" connects the research to the reason for writing.
- **Check every fact before using it.** One wrong detail makes the whole email feel insincere. If you can't verify something, leave it out.
- **Write about their problem, not the user's company.** The buyer cares about the problem. The product matters only as the way to solve it.
- **For executives, state the outcome as revenue, cost, or risk.**
- **Use a customer story only when it's real and close to the recipient** in size, industry, and problem. Never invent one. If the user hasn't given one, leave a placeholder and flag it.
- **Skip "did you know" statistics.** A teaching tone lowers reply rates.
- **Use hedge words such as "might", "seems", and "could".** A hypothesis framed as a guess is easy to correct, and a correction is a reply.

### Ask for a reply, not a meeting

- **Aim for a reply.** That is the only goal of a cold email, so don't ask for a call or a meeting yet.
- **Make one ask.** Emails with several asks are on Lavender's list of what doesn't work.
- **Make the question easy to answer,** such as a yes or no, or a choice between two options. Avoid open-ended questions.

### Subject line

- **Use 1 to 3 words, and keep it boring.** It should read like an internal email between colleagues.
- **Leave out verbs, numbers, questions, and first names.** "Support hiring" works. "Quick question about scaling support?" doesn't.

### Sending in volume

If the user sends a batch, keep it to 50 cold emails a day or fewer, spaced out over the day, to protect deliverability.

### Example

```
Subject: Support hiring

Hi Dana,

I saw Northwind is hiring three support engineers this quarter.

That might mean ticket volume is growing faster than the team can answer it. [Real customer of similar size] cut first-response time in half with us.

Is response time a priority this quarter?
```

## Replies and notes

Replies use the user's own voice, not Lavender's format.

### Voice

- **Write 3 to 5 short paragraphs of full sentences** in active voice, using "I".
- **Inside an existing thread, skip the greeting.** Start with the content, not "Hi X".
- **Avoid lines that sound machine-written,** such as "is exactly the kind of problem I…", "speaks for itself", and "easy to root for".
- **Avoid short punchy filler** such as "That's on me." or "It meant a lot."
- **Don't use exclamation marks, buzzwords, semicolons, or em dashes.**
- **Keep praise simple and true,** such as "the founders' track record is outstanding". Never add specifics, statistics, or funding facts unless they come from a source the user gave or one you checked. Unverified claims are what the user most often has to strip from drafts.

### Situations

- **Late reply.** Open with a thanks or an apology that gives a real reason, for example: "I have too many unreads piled up these days, and important messages sometimes slip through."
- **Decline.** State the decision plainly and give the real reason. If the user hasn't said why, ask. Close with "If things change for any reason, I'd love to stay in touch." When the message is about a role, offer to share it with colleagues.
- **Referral.** Write "I'll reach out to a few colleagues from [specific past job] and will connect you if they are interested." Don't promise a direct introduction, because the colleagues may not want one.
- **Recruiters.** Decline politely by default.
- **Founders.** If a founder personally asks to meet, offer to meet and to help them hire, even when the user is declining a role.

### Example

A decline to a founder who asked to meet:

```
Thank you for thinking of me for the Head of Product role. I'm not looking to move right now, because I'm in the middle of a project I want to see through.

I'd still be glad to meet, and I'm happy to help you hire for the role. I'll reach out to a few colleagues from [specific past job] and will connect you if they are interested.

If things change for any reason, I'd love to stay in touch.
```

## Before anything is sent

Show the draft first, as a draft card where the tool supports one, such as a Gmail or Slack draft. Never send a message without the user's approval of that exact text. If the user asks for a change, show the new version and get approval again.

## Output

1. **The draft,** ready to paste, with a subject line for a new email.
2. **The word count,** for a cold email.
3. **Open items:** any fact you couldn't verify and any placeholder to fill, listed after the draft instead of guessed inside it.
